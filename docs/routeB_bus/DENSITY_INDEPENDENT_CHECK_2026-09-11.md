The formulas have their analytic removable interpretations where numerator and denominator both vanish. For n>=2 put

    lambda_n=pi(n^2-1), t_n=sqrt((n^2+1)/2),
    P_n=16n^4/[pi^2(n^2-1)^2 t_n^2],
    a_n=P_n sin^2(pi*t_n),
    b_n=P_n[-sin(pi*t_n)cos(pi*t_n)/(2t_n)
             +sin^2(pi*t_n)(1/(2pi*t_n^2)+2/(pi(n^2-1))-3/(2pi*n^2))].

Then the candidate exact identity is

    H(u)=u-3/(4pi)+sum_(n>=2)(a_n*u+b_n) exp(-lambda_n*u).      (DC2)

Here is a complete proposed inversion argument. Put b^2=1-z/pi and a^2=(b^2+1)/2. Because F is entire, these square-root names introduce no branch singularities into DC1. The ratio of its F factors is (b/a)sin(pi*a)/sin(pi*b), interpreted at removable points. The inequality

    |Im a|^2=(|b^2+1|-Re(b^2+1))/4 <= |Im b|^2/2

follows from |b^2+1|<=|b|^2+1. Choose a Bromwich line Re z=c>2pi. For R=N+1/2 close it to the left with Re z=pi(1-R^2), Im z=+/-pi*R^2. On the left side, writing b=x+iy with x>=0 gives x^2-y^2=R^2. If |y|<1/4, then R<=x<=R+1/(32R), so x stays away from integers and |sin(pi*b)| is bounded below uniformly for large N. Otherwise the sinh(pi|y|) lower bound, together with |Im a|<=|y|/sqrt2, bounds the sine ratio uniformly. On the horizontal sides |y| is bounded below by a positive constant times R: x^2-y^2 is between 1-c/pi and R^2, and 2|xy|=R^2. Also |b/a| is uniformly bounded on these sides for large R. Thus |L_H(z)|<=K/|z|^2 there, for one N-independent K.
